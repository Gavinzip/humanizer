# Match the Speaker From Evidence

Read when the user supplies writing or speech samples, asks for their own voice, or wants to calibrate the writing together. Use a small working voice brief, sized to the available material. This is a content-editing method; it does not require the Speaker Toolkit vault, a new service, or a minimum number of past talks.

## Choose the Right Evidence

Use samples the user identifies as theirs or wants to emulate. Prefer material from a comparable audience and setting. A lab-meeting explanation and a marketing keynote can have different voices even from the same person.

| Source | What it can support | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Authored prose | Vocabulary, stance, sentence structure, explanation habits | Actual spoken pacing, pauses or audience response |
| Transcript with speaker labels | Spoken phrasing, examples, transitions, verbal habits | Physical delivery; timings if the transcript is untimed |
| Slides or authored notes | Intended order, terminology, planned explanation | What was actually said or understood |
| Recording with usable timing | Observed delivery in that recording | A universal speaking rate or successful comprehension |

Do not treat embedded quotations, co-speakers, transcription errors, or unaccepted AI drafts as the user's voice. If speaker attribution is unclear, limit conclusions to the attributable passages. If a supplied source cannot be read, say which evidence is unavailable; do not silently replace it with an inferred style.

When the user edits an assistant draft and says that this is how they would write it, use the changes and explicitly endorsed retained wording as local preference evidence. The whole passage does not become independently user-authored. A detector score adds no evidence about which wording they prefer.

## Build a Working Voice Brief

Extract only observations that will change the rewrite. For substantial material, keep a compact table internally or in the existing draft notes:

| Feature | Observation | Source location | Application and limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Explanation order | Starts with a concrete question before naming a method | Sample A, paragraph 2 | Reuse the ordering when the new concept benefits |
| Vocabulary | Keeps a technical term stable after defining it | Sample B, lines 4–9 | Do not cycle synonyms for variety |

The rows above illustrate the format, not a pre-filled profile. Infer observations from the actual samples. Useful features include:

- How the speaker opens a topic and gets to the point.
- How they explain an unfamiliar idea and choose examples.
- How they connect evidence to a conclusion or express uncertainty.
- Sentence length, everyday vocabulary, technical terms, and transition habits.
- Relationship between slide text and explanation, when both are available.
- Humor, interaction, and delivery rhythm only where the source supports them.

Keep recurring observations separate from one-off choices. A single passage can guide a local edit, but does not establish a permanent habit. User corrections override an inferred preference. Do not assign personality traits or invent confidence scores.

For a short sample, keep the brief to the few observable habits; do not force a profile schema. If there is no sample, proceed in a plain register appropriate to the audience. For an explicit voice-matching request, state that the draft is not yet calibrated to the speaker and ask for a relevant sample only if it would materially change the work.

Do not create or update a persistent profile simply because this guide mentions one. Use an existing authorized profile if available; saving new long-term observations follows the user's memory rules and must distinguish confirmed preferences from inference.

## Calibrate Through User Feedback

Use this mode when the user asks to tune the voice together. Choose the relevant use case from context; ask one focused question if the audience or medium would materially change the writing. With no author sample, calibration can still begin from a short passage in the conversation. Mark agent-written passages as candidates, not user-authored evidence.

- Keep the passage's factual content, uncertainty, and intended action fixed. If no concrete correction is available, offer two or three credible alternatives that vary a useful feature such as directness, conversational distance, or explanation order. When the user has already supplied their version, work from it and show one application to a new passage. Do not make a deliberately clumsy option so the polished one wins, or turn technical writing into slang to demonstrate a difference.
- Ask which wording feels right or where the reader hesitates. Let the user select parts, rewrite a sentence, or reject every version. A preference for one example does not establish the whole voice.
- Turn the feedback into a narrow editorial observation: what was accepted or rejected, for which audience and use, with the source passage. Distinguish explicit feedback from an inference. Avoid vague labels such as "more human" as the only rule.
- Apply the observation to a different short passage in the same use case, or to the user's next real draft. Check whether it still helps before treating it as a broader preference. Do not rerun the same choice question after the answer is clear or require a fixed number of rounds.
- Keep different registers separate. A casual chat preference does not automatically govern client correspondence, research prose, or presentation scripts. An explicit request for a shared default may guide those uses, but factual fidelity and the current audience still apply.
- When skill or memory updates are authorized, persist only confirmed reusable preferences with their scope. Keep rejected and unreviewed candidates out of the personal profile. A local wording correction should not become a universal forbidden-word rule.

Deliver the current short trial. Ask a focused feedback question only when its answer would change the next edit; do not end every turn with another calibration questionnaire. Do not append a full critique or a claim of detector resistance. User acceptance establishes whether the passage feels right; package validation only establishes that the skill is structurally usable.

Use the user's edits and acceptance to evaluate each trial. Follow [feedback-learning.md](feedback-learning.md) to preserve confirmed preferences with scope, evidence, and acceptance state. A selected correction is evidence in that context, not an automatic global writing rule. Optional external measurements follow [detector-checks.md](detector-checks.md) and require the user's authorization.

## Apply the Brief

Reproduce explanatory habits before repeating signature phrases. If a speaker uses concrete questions, identify a real question supported by the new topic. Do not attach their favorite opening to every slide. Do not transplant a joke, anecdote, emotional reaction, or claim from an unrelated talk.

Use connective language where it helps a listener follow reasoning. A phrase such as 「先看左邊」 is useful when the visual is known; do not remove it as generic signposting, and do not invent a left/right relationship when no layout was inspected.

Preserve the degree of certainty the material supports. A confident speaker can confidently explain that the evidence is limited. Personal style never supplies missing evidence.

## Review

Compare the draft with the source sample, not with an imagined ideal speaker:

- Does the explanatory order resemble the sample where relevant?
- Have formal terms been replaced with unfamiliar slang or marketing language?
- Are the familiar phrases organically placed, or repeated as a costume?
- Has a reaction, anecdote, or opinion appeared that the speaker never supplied?
- Does any claim about pace or delivery lack recording evidence?

When showing a calibration result, name the few habits actually used and keep unsupported observations unknown. A text review can establish these editorial choices; user acceptance establishes whether the voice feels right.
